# 1. Philosophy

Every cold email we send must do one thing: make the reader feel like we actually know them. Not generic. Not salesy. Like a friend who happens to have exactly what they need, reaching out at exactly the right moment.

We sell dolomite. But we are not selling dolomite. We are selling trust, reliability, and the confidence that switching — or adding — a supplier will not be a headache.

The SMYKM (Show Me You Know Me) framework is at the heart of every email. Combined with deep company research, this is what separates a reply from a delete.

From v7: the field work this handbook is built on showed one thing over and over — the emails that got rejected weren't the ones with bad research. They were the ones that made a claim we couldn't fully stand behind: an assumption about the prospect's situation, a stale news item dressed as current, a hook that touched a tragedy or a labor dispute, or a phrase reused so many times it started to sound templated. Good research is necessary but not sufficient. Discipline in what you don't say is just as important as what you do.

> **✓** ***v9:*** *the professional-sales literature agrees the deciding factor is relevance to a real, specific signal, not stylistic tricks or forced "personality." A rougher, less-polished sentence that comes from genuine research reads as more trustworthy than a perfectly smooth one that doesn't — see 6.4.*

# 2. The Research Protocol — Claude Does This First

Before writing a single word, Claude must conduct deep research on both the person and the company. This is not optional. The quality of the research determines the quality of the email.

## 2.1 Research: The Human (LinkedIn)

- Full name, current role, company, location
- Complete career path — every role, every company, every transition
- Education — school, degree, location
- LinkedIn posts, articles, comments — what do they write about?
- Certifications, awards, volunteer work
- The story: what is the narrative arc of their career? Is there a pivot? A pattern? A surprising connection?

> **✓** *the best personal hooks come from unexpected career pivots, unusual first jobs, volunteer work, obscure certifications, or LinkedIn posts that reveal how they think. The more obscure and specific, the better — a hook that anyone could recognize is not a hook, it's a description.*

## 2.1a Screenshot & Data Verification — Standing Practice

This is a mandatory step before every send, not something done only when asked to "validate." Errors here (a misread date, a stale figure, a wrong job title) are the kind of mistake that is embarrassing in a live email — by the time the person notices, the damage is done.

- If a screenshot or exported PDF is low-resolution or the text layer doesn't extract cleanly, re-render it at 200 DPI (not the default \~72–96 DPI) before giving up — this alone resolves most illegible sections.
- Cross-check every number, date, and proper noun against at least one independent source before it goes in an email.
- If a source is still genuinely unclear after re-rendering, say so explicitly in your own notes and to the team — never present an uncertain read as a verified fact.

## 2.1b NEW (v10) — CRM vs. LinkedIn Conflict

The CRM is a lead list, not a verified fact — a plant or company note can go stale the moment someone changes role, or can simply be wrong (e.g. a plant listed under the wrong group company). When the CRM's note about a prospect's company/plant conflicts with what their current LinkedIn shows:

- The current LinkedIn wins — address the prospect at the role and scope LinkedIn shows now, not the CRM's note.
- Never silently split the difference or write around the conflict — pick the verified current fact and flag the discrepancy plainly to the team in the same message as the draft.
- If the CRM-listed plant doesn't actually appear among the parent company's known plants (a quick check of the group's own site resolves this), treat that as a sign the CRM note is simply outdated, not as a reason to invent a bridge between the two.

> **ℹ** ***Field example:*** *Marc Scheidegger's CRM entry read "Euroglas - Lommel," but Lommel isn't one of Euroglas's actual plants, and his current LinkedIn showed him in a Head of Procurement role at the Glas Trösch group level since several months earlier. The email addressed him at the group level, on the group-level fact, and the Lommel discrepancy was flagged to the team rather than guessed at.*

## 2.2 Research: The Company — Use This Hierarchy

Always follow this priority order. Never skip a level. Never invent facts.

| Priority | Level | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Best | Plant-specific news | New furnace at Verallia Wirges; Josephine at HEINZ-GLAS Kleintettau; furnace reconstruction at a prospect's exact plant (e.g. SORG rebuild at Prachinburi Glass Industry, 180→215t/day) |
| 2 — Good | Country-specific news | Verallia Deutschland furnace renewal; O-I Andean region Q1 growth |
| 3 — OK | Group-level / international news | Verallia Fit to Win; Saint-Gobain €1B India commitment; a parent company's rebrand/ownership change (e.g. O-I BJC Vietnam → BJC Glass Vietnam) |
| 4 — Fallback | The prospect's own stated role/mandate, or a verified static plant fact | Their LinkedIn text literally says they're tasked with reducing monosourcing; or a verified furnace count/capacity with no recent news attached — use it as a plain fact, never framed as "this is what your job means" |
| ✗ Never | Invented, unverified, stale-dressed-as-current, or negative/sensitive facts | No fabrication. No assumption. Never use a labour dispute, layoffs, a disaster with casualties, an active operational crisis, an insolvency itself, or negative financial results (revenue/profit decline) as a hook — see 7. |

## 2.3 Research: The Space (Industry)

- Is there a local dolomite supplier in their country? (Italy, Portugal, Austria — yes. Middle East — less so.)
- Current glass industry trends affecting their business
- Regional supply chain challenges

## 2.4 Fact Verification — Mandatory

- Cross-reference LinkedIn dates with company website/news
- If a furnace rebuild is mentioned — confirm which plant, when, capacity numbers
- If a plant is mentioned — confirm it belongs to this person's scope
- If pricing or volume is mentioned — confirm it is accurate

> **⚠** *Never include an unverified fact. If you are not sure — leave it out. One wrong detail destroys credibility.*

## 2.5 Recency Verification

A search result's "age" metadata is not proof of when the news actually happened. Republished or syndicated copies of old articles can carry fresh-looking dates.

- Before treating anything as "current" (within 6 months), find at least two independent sources or cross-check internal consistency (e.g. an anniversary date should match the math).
- If a fact is a round-number anniversary or a "recently announced" investment, search specifically for the original announcement date, not just the topic.
- If a fact is borderline (roughly 6 months old, e.g. 6.3 months), it's fine to use if it's genuinely plant-specific and the best available — but say so plainly rather than silently rounding it into "recent."
- If you cannot confirm recency after a reasonable search, say so plainly rather than presenting a guess as verified.
- A fresh-looking publish date is not proof on its own — check whether the underlying event (an acquisition, a merger) actually happened years earlier and was simply republished or re-indexed; when in doubt, use the static/structural fact instead and flag it as such rather than the dressed-up "news."

> **✗** ***Field trap caught:*** *an article reporting "CPC acquires Sphinx Glass for $190M" carried a fresh search-index date, but the acquisition itself had actually happened in 2013/2014 — a decade-old, republished article. Not used; a multiply-sourced static fact (600 tons/day float glass capacity) was used instead.*

## 2.6 Technical-Fit Vigilance — Confirm a Real Furnace Exists

A prospect's name, SIC code, or self-description is not proof they melt raw batch (i.e. actually need dolomite). Confirm before drafting:

- Distinguish a genuine primary glass manufacturer (own melting furnace) from a downstream processor/trader — tempering, laminating, lampworking ("cracking-off" equipment is a lampworking term, not a melting one), or a merchant reselling flat glass.
- Distinguish the real melting entity from a country/regional sales, labeling, or decoration subsidiary of a foreign-melting parent (e.g. a US distribution arm whose furnace actually sits with a Chinese parent company).
- If a company's own materials contradict each other ("self-owned factory" in one place, "global trading company" / "multi-factory sourcing" in another), the fit is genuinely uncertain — don't resolve the ambiguity in your own favor. Use the qualifying-question approach (8.2) instead of an assumed pain point, even once a named contact (up to CEO level) is found — a polished LinkedIn bio repeating the same marketing language does not resolve the ambiguity.
- If uncertain after reasonable research, flag it to the team before drafting rather than sending a full pitch to a wrong-fit account.

> **✗** ***NEW (v11) — confirmed by a real reply:*** *AGC Glass Zeebrugge replied plainly: "As I'm plant manager of the factory in Zeebrugge, where we produce mirrors, we have no need of dolomite. This is for float factories. We buy the glass to transform in mirrors." The company name ("AGC Glass") and the group (a real float glass producer elsewhere) both looked like a fit — only the specific plant didn't. Always confirm the plant itself melts raw batch, not just that the group does somewhere.*

# 3. Personalization — What NOT to Do

| The Mistake | Example | Why It Fails |
|---|---|---|
| Explaining their job back to them | "Reducing monosourcing is literally part of what you do" | Reads as telling them their own job — even sourced from their own LinkedIn text, restating it as a lecture is patronizing |
| Implicit judgment with no "you" | "A job most keep for decades" (about a company they left) | No second-person pronoun, but still an evaluative claim about their choice to leave — same pattern as a backhanded compliment |
| CV-list opener | "Job A, then Job B, then Job C, then Job D" — even at 2 stops if every stop is fully named | Reads as a résumé summary, not a story — see 4③ for the fix (one vivid detail + time-jump + contrast pointe) |
| Generic pain point | "Most companies struggle with…" | Reads as spam — could be sent to anyone |
| Attack framing | "After your tough year financially…", referencing a revenue/profit decline | Reads as an insult about their company's results |
| Joining pain point and value prop | "…open to a new partnership? If so, we own the mine…" | Makes the value prop sound conditional on their answer — keep them as two separate paragraphs, always |
| Fake-sounding helpfulness | "We'd love to help" dropped next to a technical fact with no real connection | Reads as corporate filler — if warmth is the goal, make it a genuine, direct question instead ("we wanted to ask, genuinely") |

# 4. Email Architecture

Every email has 8 components. Each has a specific job. Do not skip any.

## ① Subject Line — The Most Important Part

Formula: [Personal Hook] + [Personal/Company Hook] + Marmara Island

- Two genuinely personal/obscure hooks (a certificate, old role, niche detail, LinkedIn post reference) — OR one personal hook + one specific, verified company fact.
- Marmara Island: always last. Non-negotiable.
- Use " + " to separate the parts.
- The subject must make zero sense to anyone except the recipient. It is a riddle, not a summary of their career.
- NEW (v10): keep it short — two hooks maximum, never three. A third hook (even a true, relevant one) makes the subject line too long for most inboxes and dilutes the riddle effect; cut the weaker of the three rather than keep all of them.

> **✗** *a subject like "Logistics to Sourcing + Ankleshwar Line 2 + Marmara Island" is too explanatory — it describes the career arc logically, which anyone could read and understand.*

> **✓** *riddle-style: "thyssenkrupp Nashik + Haldyn Glass Gujarat + Marmara Island", "Al-Azhar Biology Degree + Cold End Chief + Marmara Island", "Computer Forensic Technician + Robotic Process Automation + Marmara Island", "Michelin Tire Internship + $907,900 Savings + Marmara Island".*

> **✗** ***v10 field correction:*** *a subject carrying three hooks (e.g. a personal detail + a referrer's name + a company fact, all before "Marmara Island") read as too long and was trimmed down to the two strongest elements.*

## ② Greeting

Always: Hi [First Name],

Exceptions (8.6): a senior/MD-level contact with no established rapport, no LinkedIn found, or — new in v10 — a LinkedIn profile too sparse to build a real opener from, uses a formal greeting instead: "Dear Mr./Ms. [Last Name]" or a local convention, e.g. "Dear Khun [First Name]" in Thailand.

Never: Dear Sir/Madam, To Whom It May Concern, Hello.

## ③ The Opener — The Second Most Important Part

The opener appears as preview text in the inbox. It determines whether they open the email.

- Facts only — no judgments, no compliments, no opinions, no second-person pronouns. Keep it in neutral third-person narrative.
- Even without a "you" in the sentence, avoid any evaluative framing of a career choice (staying, leaving, pivoting) — state what happened, not what it says about them.
- Tell their career STORY. Never list their jobs. Max 2 clean, distinct beats — when in doubt, cut a stop rather than cram in a third.
- The lesson learned repeatedly in the field: naming every stop in sequence ("Job A, then Job B, then Job C") reads as a CV list even at 2–3 stops. The fix — lead with one vivid, specific detail (a year, a company name), take a time-jump instead of naming every intermediate stop ("Thirteen years later…"), and land on a short contrast pointe.
- Max 2–3 sentences.
- Must end with a pointe — a short, punchy bridge that connects their story to why we're reaching out, specific to them, never a generic truism.
- NEW (v10): the "skip the story, open with the company intro" fallback is no longer just for senior/no-rapport contacts (8.6) — it applies any time the LinkedIn profile genuinely doesn't give two clean career beats to work with (a single job, a single line of education, no narrative arc), whatever the person's seniority. Forcing a 2-beat story out of a 1-line profile produces a thin, speculative opener — better to open directly and lean on a strong company/plant fact instead.

> **✓** *(time-jump, not a chain): "Production Engineer at thyssenkrupp in 2012. Thirteen years and stints at two of India's biggest glass makers later — now Assistant Manager, Purchase & Production Planning at Majan Glass in Oman. From German machinery to Omani furnaces."*

> **✓** *(image over chain): "Thyssenkrupp's shop floor in Nashik, 2012. Thirteen years and two of India's biggest glass names later, the view is furnaces in Oman — Assistant Manager, Purchase & Production Planning at Majan Glass."*

> **✗** ***Corrected in the field:*** *"A job most keep for decades" (describing the previous employer) reads as a judgment of their choice to leave, even with no "you" present. Replaced with a plain time-jump instead.*

> **ℹ** ***v10 field examples:*** *Markus Poeschl (Stölzle Köflach) and Nicolas Huang (Eurofloat) both had very sparse profiles — one job, one line of education, no visible arc. Both openers skipped the story entirely and opened on the role/company instead, rather than inventing a second beat.*

> **✓** *NEW (v11) — the personalized opener is directly confirmed by real prospects, not just assumed to work: Julien Lor (Gerresheimer Momignies) replied "Wouah, you analyze perfectly my profil." Abdulrahman Yamout (Gulf Glass Manufacturing) opened his reply with "You have a great attractive introduction of mine while starting this conversation 😊." Do not cut the opener to save words when trimming toward the 95–199-word target (6.3) — cut elsewhere first.*

> **✓** ***NEW (v11.1) — from the CRM:*** *NEW (v11.1) — keep explicit years and "since" out of the opener. In the CRM, openers containing a year replied 6.8% against 25.6% without one (month-controlled, p=0.003). Say durations ("eleven years at...", "three years researching...") and lead with the most unexpected true detail instead of a dated chain. Existing examples in section 11 that contain years are kept for history, not as the model to copy.*

> **✓** ***NEW (v11.1) — from the CRM:*** *NEW (v11.1) — pick the buyer. Where several people at one site could be contacted, prefer the purchasing/procurement contact (non-senior buyers and purchasing managers replied about twice as often as plant or senior roles). When writing to a plant manager anyway, expect a lower rate and make the mail easy to forward.*

## ④ Company Intro — One Sentence, Always the Same

*I'm [Niels/Dili] from Medas Dolomite. We mine and ship high-purity dolomite from our own port on Marmara Island, Turkey — only for glass manufacturers.*

## ⑤ Pain Point — Its Own Paragraph

This is where most emails go wrong. Rules:

- Never explain implications back to the reader.
- Never assert a claim about the prospect's specific situation or feelings.
- Never tell them what their own job or company means ("X is literally part of what you do") — even when the words come from their own LinkedIn text, restating it that way reads as a lecture.
- Use a verified plant/country/group-level fact (2.2), their own stated mandate framed as a plain fact (not a lecture), or an open question.
- 1–2 sentences. Always ends with a closing bridge sentence — rotate the wording, but always include one.

### 4a. The Default Pain-Point Template — REVERSED IN v11

> **⚠** *the v9/v10 default ended the pain point on an explicit question. Month-controlled CRM data points toward a statement close (16.1% against 5.3%, OR 4.2, p=0.08 — only 38 question-close emails in the clean set), so a statement stays the default, but this is directional, not proven. The earlier 18.9% / 9.5% and 42.9% / 13.3% figures were inflated by send-month differences. The question-based template stays as the v10 variant, and 8.2 qualifying questions are unaffected.*

New v11 default — a statement, no question mark, using the "Here's why I'm reaching out:" lead-in that was already the long-format standard:

*"Here's why I'm reaching out: with [Company] running [verified furnace/capacity fact], [the stakes or implication, stated as a plain fact — e.g. 'one off-spec batch can shut everything down' / 'a hiccup in raw material supply isn't something a line running that hot can afford']. [optional bridge: 'We thought it was the right moment to introduce ourselves.']"*

The invitation moves to the CTA paragraph instead, where it belongs — and the data has a mild preference for keeping it conditional there too ("If that sounds useful, happy to send a sample" replied slightly better than an unconditional "Happy to send our TDS" — 16.8% vs. 12.2%, a softer signal than the two points above but consistent with it):

- State the fact and its stakes/implication as something true, not as a pitch — the reader should feel informed, not sold to.
- Still no implicit judgment of the reader (3, 4③) — the stakes belong to the plant/company, not to a claim about what the reader personally feels or should do.
- Keep the fact and its implication in the same flowing sentence or two — pain point and value prop stay separate paragraphs, as before.
- The v10 question-based phrasing ("are you open to a new supplier, if we can offer good quality and price?") remains available for 8.2's genuine qualifying questions — where the open question IS the point — but is retired as the default pain-point close.

> **✓** ***Field example — statement close:*** *"When you're running 38,000 m² of mirrors and painted glass daily, one late shipment or off-spec batch can shut everything down. That's why plants like Şişecam keep us as a second source — when things don't go as planned, we're your Plan B." (AGC Zeebrugge — wrong technical fit as it turned out, but the close itself is the model: a fact, its stakes, no question mark.)*

> **✓** ***Field example — statement close, real reply:*** *"15 years at Ardagh running logistics across EU & US — not many people have that kind of cross-border experience. Now you're building Ciner's supply chain from scratch. First furnace going live in Q2, and 650 tonnes a day from day one is no small number. ... Wanted to reach out and see if that makes sense for Lommel too." (Ciner Glass, Robert Bevers — replied, became a full sample-and-logistics conversation.)*

### Fallback Bridge (No Fresh Hook Exists)

*"With raw material costs and supply conditions shifting the way they have lately, we figured it was a good moment to ask — are you looking for a reliable backup supplier, or open to a new partnership? If so, we'd love to be that for you."*

## ⑥ Value Proposition — Separate Paragraph, Fixed Wording

*"We own the mine and the port — quality controlled from source to ship. Plants like Şişecam and others across [region] choose Marmara because the purity is consistent, the volumes are flexible and the prices are hard to beat."*

- Şişecam and Europe are always mentioned — Şişecam is the only customer Medas can name publicly; other plants can be mentioned by country but not named.
- Wording may vary occasionally instead of being copy-pasted verbatim every time, as long as the substance (mine + port ownership, purity, flexibility, price) stays.
- Never joined to the pain point by "if so" or similar — always its own paragraph.
- Never say "quarry" — always "mine."

## ⑦ Call to Action — One, Never More

*"Happy to send our TDS — or go straight to a sample, whatever works best for you! If it's easier, we're also happy to go through this directly with your purchasing department."*

## ⑧ PS — The Glass Under Pressure Report

*Full (v7/long format): "PS: Every year we put together our Glass Under Pressure report — a data-driven look at where glass margins, energy costs and raw material pricing are heading. The 2026 edition just came out. Şişecam uses it for their annual market review. Happy to send it your way!"*

*Condensed (v8/short format): "PS: Our 2026 Glass Under Pressure report is out — used by Şişecam for their market review. Happy to send it your way."*

# 5. The Three Approaches

- Approach A — Backup: new in role, new plant, new ownership, capacity scaling, a furnace startup. Angle: reliability and supply continuity.
- Approach B — Cost + Quality: cost discipline, a procurement-focused role, margin pressure. Angle: competitive pricing and consistent quality.
- Approach C — Combined: expansion plus cost pressure, multi-plant operations. Angle: lead with cost/quality, close with backup reliability.

# 6. Writing Rules

## 6.1 Tone Calibration by Profile

- Plant Director / Technician — short, direct.
- Procurement Manager — analytical, cost logic.
- Purchasing Director — professional, strategic, supply-chain framing.

## 6.2 Bridge-Phrase Rotation

Rotate among: "we figured it was worth introducing ourselves", "this seemed like the right moment to get on your radar", "we thought it was a good time to reach out" — don't overuse one, especially across prospects who share a parent group. The pain-point close itself is no longer a rotation of two question phrasings (see 4a, REVERSED in v11) — it is now a statement by default — so what rotates is the surrounding bridge language and the specific fact/implication used, not a question template.

## 6.3 Body Length — Fixed Target, No A/B Test (CHANGED IN v11)

> **⚠** *v11 retires the short/long A/B test by decision. Corrected in v11.1: once send month is controlled, length shows no measurable effect (95–199 words vs. everything else, OR \~1.0), so the earlier 32.6% vs. 12.3% claim is withdrawn. Medium length stays the single format because the user chose it and the data does not argue against it.*

- Fixed target: 95–199 words in the body, every email, no short-format (v8, 75–90 words) option anymore.
- Structure: full sentences throughout, the default pain-point paragraph ends on a statement (4a), value prop and CTA as usual, full PS wording — this is simply the old "long format" made the only format.
- High-value or genuinely complex accounts (large industrial groups, long sales cycles) may run longer than 199 words if the context is truly needed for the decision — a judgment call, not the default.
- Never exceed roughly 200 words without a clear reason — the same ceiling as before.
- This replaces the old per-day split (2 short / 2 long) — every send in a batch now targets the same 95–199-word range.

## 6.4 Warmth Without Sounding Fake

A person can ask to make an email "warmer" or "more helpful" without it becoming salesy filler. What actually reads as warm:

- A genuine, direct question ("we wanted to ask, genuinely") rather than a stock helpful-sounding phrase ("we'd love to help") dropped next to a hard technical fact — the mismatch between soft language and a hard number is what makes it feel fake.
- A real, true observation — congratulating someone on a promotion you've actually verified is fine; inventing warmth is not.
- Slightly rougher, less polished phrasing over a perfectly smooth corporate sentence — see 1, professional consensus is that imperfection reads as more human and more trustworthy.
- Not turning a request for warmth into padding — a warmer email can still hit the target word count; warmth is a register change, not an excuse to add filler sentences.

## 6.5 Pricing (When Applicable)

- Watch margin as a percentage of the base price, not just a flat €/tonne figure.
- For large volumes, present the total-commission math (margin × annual volume ÷ people splitting it) so the number means something.

# 7. Absolute Rules — Never Break

- Never position Medas as the primary/main supplier — second source, until asked otherwise.
- Never send a calendar link in the first email.
- Never use news older than 6 months without flagging it as borderline/old.
- Always "Şişecam" with the Ş.
- Max one CTA per email.
- Target length: see 6.3 — fixed at 95–199 words, every email. No short-format (v8) option anymore.
- Never fabricate a hook (e.g. never claim "a shipment heading to your region" without it being true).
- Never use a labor dispute, layoffs, a disaster with casualties, a wildfire, a sabotage accusation, or an active operational crisis (e.g. a fuel-shortage furnace shutdown, or an insolvency itself) as a hook.
- Never use negative financial results (a revenue or profit decline) as a hook — it reads as an attack on their situation, even when accurate and sourced.
- A resolved crisis (e.g. a company rescued and renamed after insolvency) can be framed positively as a fresh start — never dwelling on the crisis itself, only the resolution.

## 7.1 Italy

Italy has its own dolomite mines (Südtirol, GD Peroglio, etc.) — the objection should be addressed proactively in the value prop, not avoided.

# 8. Special Cases

## 8.1 Şişecam Group Prospects Outside Turkey

State plainly that Medas already supplies Şişecam's Turkish operations, not yet this country's entity. Value prop reframes Şişecam as "the same purity/reliability their Turkish plants already count on, not yet landed on their side of the group."

## 8.2 Technical-Fit Uncertainty

Two distinct situations, two different responses:

- Borosilicate / Type I tubing glass (e.g. FIOLAX, Gerresheimer/SCHOTT tubing) typically uses boric oxide flux, not dolomite/lime — use an honest qualifying question instead of an assumed pain point.
- Ambiguous self-description (own materials contradict each other — see 2.6) — same approach, an honest qualifying question, even once a named, senior contact (up to CEO) is identified. A polished bio repeating the same marketing language doesn't resolve the ambiguity.

> **ℹ** ***Field example:*** *"With Feemio serving 10,000+ clients across 60 countries, we wanted to ask — does your own factory melt glass from raw materials, or do you work with partner mills for that part?"*

## 8.3 No Person/Email Found — Only a Generic Address (info@, plant@, ir@)

v8's version padded the email with extra value prop to make it "forward-worthy." In the field this read as presuming to know who'd read it. The v9 version is shorter and more honest: introduce Medas in one line, then explicitly ask the reader to forward it to whoever handles raw material purchasing.

*"Hi,*

*We're Medas Dolomite — we mine and ship high-purity dolomite for glass manufacturers from our own port on Marmara Island, Turkey.*

## 8.4 A Real Named Contact, Wrong Department/Business Unit

Different from 8.3: here we know exactly who the person is and that their actual scope has nothing to do with glass raw materials (e.g. HR at a glass company; a group procurement engineer whose accounts are steel, power, or oil & gas, not glass). Two things make this worth a bespoke, no-pitch message rather than skipping entirely:

- They may be positioned to redirect us internally (an HR contact in the right company; a group-level procurement colleague who knows the glass-division buyer).
- A full SMYKM pitch to someone with zero purchasing relevance wastes the research and can cost trust if they notice the mismatch.

Approach: no SMYKM opener, no pain point, no value prop — just a short, warm, honest forward-request naming the specific business unit we're trying to reach.

*"Hi [Name],*

*Hope you don't mind the reach-out — we're Medas Dolomite, a raw material supplier for glass manufacturers based in Turkey.*

*We're trying to get in touch with whoever handles raw material purchasing at [Company/Business Unit]. Would you be able to point us in the right direction, or forward this along?*

## 8.5 Repetition Awareness — Shared Parent Groups

When multiple prospects share a parent group (e.g. two contacts at the same BJC subsidiary, or two at Majan Glass), vary the quote, framing sentence, company-fact phrasing, and opener structure each time — never send near-identical wording to two people who might compare notes internally. This also governs the Second-Round Contact Strategy (8.10) and any repeat touch at the same company.

## 8.6 Senior/MD-Level Contacts With No Rapport, No LinkedIn Found, or a Sparse Profile

UPDATED (v10) — scope widened: use a formal greeting ("Dear Mr./Ms. [Last Name]", or a local convention such as "Dear Khun [First Name]" in Thailand) instead of "Hi [First Name]" for a senior/MD-level contact with no established rapport. If no career data exists at all, or the LinkedIn profile is too sparse to give two clean career beats (one job, one line of education, no visible arc — see 4③), skip the SMYKM opener rather than force one — open directly with the company intro, regardless of the contact's seniority.

## 8.7 Breakup Email

After 2–3 unanswered attempts: a short, no-blame email leaving the door open.

## 8.8 Referral / Forwarded Emails

Thank briefly, write a fresh email to the new colleague, reference the referral naturally, and CC the original contact.

## 8.9 Follow-Up Cadence

- Follow-up 1: after 5–7 days.
- Follow-up 2: another 5–7 days later — short check-in.
- Follow-up 3: the breakup email (8.7).

## 8.10 NEW (v10) — Second-Round Contact Strategy

Applies to the large-scale re-contact round: re-approaching the roughly 300 companies already contacted once, where the first contact never replied, this time through a different person at the same company.

### Default

- Write a fresh cold mail exactly as if this were a first contact — no mention anywhere of the earlier outreach to someone else at the company.
- Mandatory variation per 8.5: a different hook, a different pain-point fact, and ideally a different format (short vs. long) than whatever was used for the first contact at that company.
- Where possible, switch the sender for the second contact (Niels ↔ Dilakshan) — two different senders reaching out independently reads as two genuine outreach attempts, not one campaign circling back.

### Exception — Clear Hierarchy Jump

When the second contact is a clear step up the hierarchy from the first (e.g. now targeting a Head of Procurement after the first attempt went to a Junior Buyer), a brief, casual mention that we'd already reached out on the operational/functional side is appropriate — it reads as diligence, not as a repeat nuisance, when the audience has clearly changed.

### Bounce vs. Non-Response

A bounced first email (the address didn't work) carries no "we were ignored" signal — it's fine to use the same sender for the second contact in that case. The sender-switch recommendation above is specifically for a first contact that was delivered and simply never answered.

> **ℹ** ***Field example:*** *Bartosz Lisowski (Guardian Glass Poland) was contacted as a same-sender second touch after the first contact (a different Guardian Glass colleague) bounced — consistent with the bounce carve-out.*

## 8.11 Reply-Handling: Real Objections — UPDATED (v11): hard no vs. soft no, plus a deferred category

When a prospect replies with a genuine objection — most commonly a local-sourcing objection ("we don't import if it's locally sourced") or a similar pushback — the reply must not become a pitch or a rebuttal.

- Respond with a short, genuinely curious, open question — never an argument for why they should still consider Medas.
- Do not re-state the second-source/backup-supplier value proposition as a counter to their objection; that reads as arguing instead of listening.
- Keep the tone warm and appreciative of their reply, not defensive.
- The goal of the reply is to learn whether there's any room at all (e.g. backup capacity for peak demand, a second source requirement elsewhere in the group) — not to win the point.

> **ℹ** ***Field example:*** *Shimaa Youssef (Saint-Gobain Egypt) raised a local-content objection. The reply contained a single open, non-argumentative question about backup/peak-demand flexibility — no pitch, no rebuttal — and was warmed up further on request without adding a second point.*

### NEW (v11) — Hard No vs. Soft No

The CRM reply review surfaced a pattern worth telling apart before deciding how to close out a thread:

- Hard no: a flat, closed statement with no opening left (e.g. "we do not use Dolomite in our process" with no further qualifier). Thank them briefly, don't push, and don't add to the thread — a breakup-style close, no follow-up push.
- Soft no: declines the current need but explicitly stays open to other opportunities or other materials (e.g. Rubin Glass: "from 3-4 years we are not using dolomite in our production. If you offer other materials we open to check them"; Monica Csaszar/Saint-Gobain Romania: "we do not use Dolomite... Keep in touch for other opportunities"). This deserves a warm, genuinely interested stay-in-touch reply — ask what materials they do use, or simply thank them and note we'll keep them posted — never written off like a hard rejection.

### NEW (v11) — Deferred Objection: Price/Logistics, Not Product

A distinct objection pattern — not a technical-fit or sourcing-policy no, but a timing/cost one — showed up from IMG Glass ("we think your price could beat our local vendor's price as mining is located in Indonesia") and Pragati Glass ("getting dolomite from turkey will be super expensive in current scenario. Lets touch base once shipping rates go back to normal"). The product itself was never in question — only freight economics at that moment.

- Do not treat this as a dead lead and do not run the standard 2-follow-up cadence (8.9) against it — the objection is about current shipping cost, not fit.
- Acknowledge the point honestly, thank them for the directness, and ask if it's fine to check back when freight rates shift.
- Flag the contact for a longer-horizon, time-triggered re-engagement outside the normal cadence (e.g. a check-in tied to a visible change in freight rates or a few months out) rather than a standard follow-up 1/2.

# 9. Price Offer Documents & Delivery Terms

> **⚠** *UPDATED (v10): Medas offers exactly two Incoterms — FOB (Marmara Island) or CFR (to the buyer's nearest port) — and no others. This is stated directly and plainly when a prospect asks about shipping/delivery terms, not just assumed internally: say clearly that these are the only two terms we work with, and that either one can be quoted the same day once they confirm which they prefer.*

- 1 page max. Two tables: specs, and pricing.
- Lead time: "To be agreed."
- Validity: 30 days standard; shorter if the supplier price is volatile — explain why.
- Bulk and Big Bag as separate rows.
- Price is never sent without verifying with Niels/Coşkun first.
- Always specify the minimum order when known.
- Any delivery/logistics specifics a prospect raises beyond the Incoterm itself (e.g. specific truck type, certificates per delivery, pre-unloading sample testing) must be confirmed with Coşkun before being promised as "standard" — don't assume operational capability on the prospect's behalf.

# 10. Pre-Send Checklist

- Every fact verified against at least one independent source ✓
- Recency of any "current" hook checked (2.5) — and any borderline-old fact flagged, not silently treated as fresh ✓
- CRM company/plant note cross-checked against current LinkedIn; current LinkedIn wins and any conflict is flagged (2.1b) ✓
- Technical fit confirmed — a real melting furnace exists and the plant belongs to this person's scope (2.6) ✓
- Opener is a story with a time-jump and a pointe, not a chain of named jobs — or, for a genuinely sparse profile, skipped in favor of a direct company-intro open (4③, 8.6) ✓
- No implicit judgment anywhere in the opener or pointe, even without "you" (3, 4③) ✓
- Subject line has at most two hooks, kept short (4①) ✓
- Pain point and value prop are separate paragraphs, never joined by "if so" ✓
- No sensitive/negative topic used as a hook (7) ✓
- Length is in the fixed 95–199 word range — no short-format option anymore (6.3) ✓
- Pain-point paragraph ends on a statement, not a question — invitation moved to the CTA instead (4a) ✓
- Tone matches the prospect's profile (Plant Director vs. Procurement vs. Global Procurement) ✓
- Bridge phrase and closing-question phrasing aren't the same ones used in the last several emails of this batch — especially for shared-parent-group prospects (8.5, 6.2) ✓
- For a second-round contact: no mention of the first attempt unless it's a clear hierarchy jump; sender switched unless the first attempt bounced (8.10) ✓
- Delivery terms, if mentioned, state only FOB or CFR — nothing else promised without Coşkun's confirmation (9) ✓
- Read it out loud — does it sound natural, like something one professional would send another? ✓
